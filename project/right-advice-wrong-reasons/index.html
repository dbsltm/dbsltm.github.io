Abstract

When people seek advice from AI on social situations, the system may recommend a decision they fully agree with but justify it with reasons they disagree with. Conversely, it may offer reasons they agree with, and yet reach a decision they reject. We distinguish decision alignment, whether an advisor recommends the option favored by a user’s measured values, from reason alignment, whether its stated reasons align with the user’s values. In this work, we ask how these two forms of alignment shape people’s preferences for AI advisors and their willingness to use them in different roles. We conducted a within-subjects study (N=121) in which participants evaluated three AI advisors varying in decision and reason alignment across eight everyday moral dilemmas. Participants then chose which advisors they would consult, allow to draft messages for review, or allow to act on their behalf in new tasks. When seeking advice, participants most often preferred an advisor that reasoned through their prioritized values while recommending the opposing decision (40%). However, when participants had to pick advisors for new tasks, their preferences shifted toward advisors that also recommended decisions aligned with their values, rather than those that only shared their reasons. Also, as participants considered tasks that gave AI advisors greater autonomy, they became more likely to opt out of using any advisor, from 12% when consulting to 38% when allowing an advisor to act on their behalf. Our findings suggest that decision and reason alignment shape advisor preferences differently across contexts of AI use, and that alignment with a user’s values does not necessarily imply willingness to delegate.

Research questions
  1. RQ1: When choosing which AI advisor to ask about a dilemma, do people favor one that shares their decision, their reasons, or both? (Section 4.1)
  2. RQ2: Does that preference hold when the advisor has a concrete role in a task, and does it change as that role gives the advisor more control? (Section 4.2)

01 / Motivation

Agreement is not the same as sharing someone's values

Agreement on a recommended decision does not necessarily imply agreement on the values used to justify it: the same recommendation can be supported by different value-based reasons. This raises the question of whether matching a person’s preferred decision is sufficient for alignment—that is, whether the AI advice is useful, reflects their underlying values and warrants their trust and reliance.

That matters more as people move from asking an AI for advice to letting it act for them. Whatever reasons an advisor gives also show how it will judge the cases a user never sees.

02 / Design

Three advisors built from each person's own values

Each participant first filled in the Portrait Values Questionnaire. For every dilemma, its two options were favored by values at opposite poles of one Schwartz axis, so a person's profile told us which option their values favor. We then wrote four replies per dilemma, crossing the two options with the two values, and showed each person three of them.

03 / Asking · RQ1

When Asking, People Prefer the Advisor That Shares Their Reasons but Not Their Decision

When deciding which advisor they would ask about a dilemma, participants selected ReaAligned in 40% of cases, compared with 31% for FullyAligned and 29% for DecAligned (Figure 1). ReaAligned was selected more often than DecAligned by 10.1% (95% CI [3.9, 16.2], p=.002) and more often than FullyAligned by 8.3% (95% CI [2.7, 13.7], p=.006).

Participants who preferred DecAligned often described it as balanced or fair (11/29 vs. 7/66 among those preferring other advisors; Fisher’s exact p=.004), with one calling it “more balanced and overall more fair.” Those preferring ReaAligned emphasized its logical, thoughtful, or thorough reasoning (9/37); one noted that it “reasoned the same way I do but flushed [sic] out my thoughts.” In contrast, participants who preferred FullyAligned emphasized shared values, similar thinking, and agreement with their own choices, with some also highlighting empathy and emotional support.

05 / Who opts out

Participants with Stronger Values Were More Likely to Opt Out of AI Assistance

We grouped participants by how far apart their two competing values were. Those with the strongest value conflicts were the most likely to choose no advisor, already when letting it draft. This analysis is exploratory.

06 / Implications

What this means for building and evaluating aligned AI

💡
People May Prefer Alternative Perspectives Grounded in Their Values

Prior work on human–AI complementarity has examined how differing perspectives contribute to advice-taking, and the tension between aligning with users’ preferences and providing complementary assistance. In our study, ReaAligned offered a countervailing recommendation, much like a devil’s advocate, while framing its justification in values the participant endorsed. Rather than simply mirroring users’ judgments, AI advisors may therefore be valued for introducing alternatives grounded in what users care about.

💡
Alignment Preferences Depend on the Advisor’s Role

When seeking advice, people retain control over whether to accept a recommendation, so they may value alternative perspectives. When considering delegating a task, though, they may attend more closely to the decisions an advisor would make on their behalf.

Beyond alignment in an advisor’s decisions and expressed reasons, other considerations may become important when people evaluate AI for consequential tasks. For example, willingness to delegate may depend on who bears responsibility for an outcome, how familiar people are with using AI for a particular task, and whether they can review or correct the AI’s actions. These factors may help explain why preferences formed when evaluating observed advice did not carry over unchanged to new tasks, and why participants were more likely to opt out of delegated action. Although our study does not isolate their individual contributions, it provides a framework for examining how alignment in decisions and expressed reasons relates to people’s willingness to use AI across different roles.

Limitations

01 · Measuring valuesAlignment is measured against value profiles, not stated choices.We operationalized personal alignment using Schwartz’s basic values, treating the higher-scoring value in each dilemma as the basis for the participant’s value-favored decision. However, people’s choices may reflect multiple values and contextual considerations not captured by the PVQ-21.
02 · Controlled responsesEight dilemmas, fixed advisor replies.We used a controlled set of eight dilemmas and predefined advisor replies to isolate alignment in recommended decisions and expressed reasons. Participants formed impressions of advisors through these responses before choosing whether to use them in subsequent tasks.
03 · Hypothetical delegationChoices had no real-world consequences.Our study measured hypothetical advisor choices rather than delegation with real-world consequences. Moreover, although the consult and act conditions used the same six scenarios, the drafting condition involved different situations adapted from earlier dilemmas. The broader comparison between asking and using an advisor also differed in task context and whether participants could see the advisors’ responses before choosing.

Future work

DeliberationMeasure deliberation, not only selection.We recorded which advisor people picked, so we cannot tell whether they sought out alternative perspectives or reconsidered their decisions.
Task and oversightTask-specific values, accountability and oversight.How these interact with decision and reasoning alignment to shape preferences for AI assistance and delegation.
InteractionOpen-ended, multi-turn interaction.Settings where users can question recommendations, clarify their values, and watch how an advisor adapts over time.
Real stakesComparable tasks with real consequences.Whether the patterns hold across matched tasks and in settings where users can observe and evaluate the outcomes of delegation.
PrioritiesWhen and why people prioritize each dimension.How priorities between decision and reasoning alignment shape willingness to use AI across tasks of different stakes.
